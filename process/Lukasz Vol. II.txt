# Meeting Summary

## Main Topics Discussed

### 1. LiFE Adolescent Crisis Service and Continuity of Care
- LiFE was described as a low-threshold service for adolescents in crisis. Admission does not require a formal psychiatric diagnosis, and its waiting list is comparatively short; admission may be possible within about **2 weeks**.
- The service aims to intervene early during a crisis and reduce the risk of problems becoming chronic.
- LiFE brings three levels of care together in one building and under the same treatment team:
  1. **Inpatient care**, typically for around **2 weeks** at the beginning of admission, when adolescents need substantial support.
  2. **Day clinic**, where adolescents receive treatment and attend the clinic school during the day, then return home at night.
  3. **Outpatient care**, continuing with the same team after transition from the day clinic.
- This model is intended to avoid difficult transitions between services with different staff. It also allows flexible escalation—for example, a day-clinic patient can return to inpatient care for one or more nights if needed.
- The inpatient stay is not a required preliminary stage for every psychiatric service; in LiFE it is commonly used because adolescents are admitted during a crisis and may initially need intensive support.

### 2. Questionnaire-Based Clinical Research
- The research accompanying the newer LiFE service has two components. The first is a questionnaire battery completed online through **REDCap** at **pre-admission**, **discharge**, and **3 months after discharge**.
- Adolescents complete measures, and questionnaires are also sent to parents; therapists complete questionnaires as well.
- Measures cover clinical factors, suicidality, symptom severity, and mental-health risks and resources, including perceived stress, childhood trauma, impulsivity, social connectedness, social support, and quality of life.
- Questionnaire results are also part of clinical routine and support therapy planning by helping teams identify symptoms, resources, and areas that may be addressed in treatment.
- Participants emphasized that psychiatric assessment relies heavily on self-report and interviews. The instruments are generally well-established, with prior use spanning roughly **20–50 years** and established psychometric properties. The team uses analytical approaches including structural equation modeling to examine hypothesized pathways, including those related to suicidality.

### 3. EMA and Smartphone Passive-Sensing Study
- The second research component is a more intensive smartphone study involving a subsample of eligible adolescents. Participation takes place in the outpatient phase and begins at least **2 weeks after transition**, because the clinical team considers the transition period potentially unstable.
- Each study period lasts **2 weeks**. Participants receive **5 email notifications per day**, each with a **1-hour** window to complete an ecological momentary assessment (EMA) survey; an unanswered survey is deactivated after that window. Recruitment is ongoing rather than simultaneous.
- EMA topics include:
  - **Context:** location, current activity, and whether the adolescent is alone or with others.
  - **Mood and well-being:** momentary mood, optimism, and life satisfaction, using established measures.
  - **Lifestyle:** sleep quality, drug use, meals, and whether meals were eaten alone or with others.
  - **Stress:** momentary stress and confidence in coping, assessed at all five daily prompts; evening questions cover daily hassles and uplifts, including specific events such as whether a painful memory arose that day.
  - **Social connectedness:** perceived connection to people around them, assessed at each prompt.
  - **Suicidal ideation:** assessed once per prompt interval, asking whether ideation occurred since the previous survey and, if so, its intensity.
- Researchers highlighted the possible relevance of accumulating small stressors and “micro-uplifts,” not only major stressful events.
- A safety alert is built into the EMA protocol: if reported suicidal-ideation intensity exceeds a set threshold, the LiFE treatment team receives an automated notification to check whether the adolescent is safe or needs more support. The alert approach was developed in response to ethics-review concerns about studying a vulnerable population.
- The study uses the **CoRa app** for EMA and passive phone sensing. Current study participants are iPhone users; Android participation may be considered, but the data available differ by operating system.
- For iPhones, the team reported collecting battery level/charging status, screen brightness and on/off status, location (with some location information available specifically when the app is in use), activity type, nearby Wi-Fi networks and connection status, Bluetooth connections, accelerometer data and step counts, and incoming-call occurrence and duration. Some planned sensors are unavailable on iPhone without Apple’s ResearchKit framework. The team believes the accelerometer data are collected regularly, potentially even when the app is not open.
- Android can provide app-use data, which is not available in the current iPhone setup. Some common measures—battery, screen status, location, Wi-Fi, and Bluetooth—are available across platforms. Step count and activity data were not captured by the Android device tested. Since the current sample consists of iPhone users, app-use data may not be usable in the present dataset.
- Recruitment is challenging: adolescents must be approached at the right time, consent discussions must be arranged, and parents also need to be informed. The process can take long enough that some interested adolescents are discharged before enrollment. The sample remains small—approximately **10 participants at most** at the time of discussion—and data collection is continuing. The team noted strict inclusion/exclusion criteria and the difficulty of recruiting this population.

### 4. Potential Analyses and Clinical Value of Passive Sensing
- Proposed research questions include whether passive sensing improves prediction of suicidality or other mental-health outcomes when added to EMA data, and whether passive data can identify patterns or markers associated with psychiatric variables.
- The clinical motivation is that suicidal ideation may be poorly communicated, even to close family or friends. Passive sensing might provide additional information that can corroborate questionnaires and interviews, rather than replace clinical assessment.
- The group discussed complementary data sources:
  - **Smartphone sensors** may capture mobility and activity patterns.
  - **Network/router data** may indicate internet activity, sleep-related periods of inactivity, irregular schedules, and broad categories of services or websites used.
  - **Acoustic sensing** (including the previously discussed iHearU project) may provide information when a person speaks, but cannot help when there is no voice activity.
- Participants stressed that each sensing modality has limitations. Router-based monitoring only sees traffic when a device is connected to the monitored Wi-Fi/access point. It cannot see activity over cellular data or an unrelated network. A VPN-based approach over mobile data was raised as technically possible but considered more complicated and not necessarily worthwhile. Lack of network traffic is ambiguous: the person may be asleep, away from home, or simply not using digital devices.
- The clinicians considered sleep particularly valuable because it can be difficult to establish adolescents’ actual sleep schedules through self-report, and sleep disruption may be relevant to depression, suicidality, mania, and psychosis. Social connectedness and loneliness were also identified as important, though digital social-network use alone cannot determine whether online activity is protective connection or a substitute for in-person relationships.
- Website or service categories may offer useful context even when content is encrypted and cannot be inspected. Examples discussed included visits to crisis-help websites, self-harm forums, social networks, music services, and gaming services. The group emphasized that such signals should be treated as supplementary indicators, not definitive evidence or a diagnosis.

### 5. Network-Data Dashboard Demonstration and Constraints
- The collaborating team demonstrated a clinician-facing dashboard using **scripted/simulated traffic profiles**, not real participant traffic. The underlying system for collecting and analyzing real-world network data is available, but the example shown was simulated.
- The prototype included:
  - Device/person configuration, such as associating a person with a phone and laptop and their network identifiers.
  - A time-based heat map of packet volume. Darker blocks represent more internet-dependent activity; gaps may indicate inactivity, sleep, or absence from home, but cannot establish which explanation is correct.
  - Broad indicators such as distinct social domains, use of particular online services, and visits to crisis or self-harm-related sites.
  - A simulated 14-day period used to establish an individual baseline, followed by a clinical period in which deviations and possible risk patterns are displayed.
- The prototype’s modeled output suggested possible suicidal ideation for one scripted profile after visits to crisis lines and self-harm forums; participants clarified that this was not an actual clinical classification from real data.
- A key technical limitation is that router-based traffic capture requires the monitored device to use the controlled Wi-Fi. This could be practical in a clinic or in a participant’s home if the system is deployed there, but it would leave gaps when the participant uses mobile data or is elsewhere.
- The team discussed whether such network data could complement smartphone sensing and clinical questionnaires. Clinicians expressed interest in a clinical-support tool, provided that risk indicators are communicated cautiously and do not purport to make diagnoses or clinical decisions autonomously.

### 6. Dashboard Design and Clinician Needs
- Clinicians welcomed the concept of presenting additional information as support for interviews and treatment, rather than as a replacement for diagnosis or professional judgment.
- A two-layer interface was proposed:
  1. A simple, quick-to-read overview with clear risk/status labels and visually accessible trends, suitable for time-constrained clinical work.
  2. A more detailed layer with underlying measures, numbers, and explanations of how indicators were derived.
- The clinicians favored showing trajectories and deviations from a person’s usual pattern—for example, changes in sleep regularity or activity—rather than treating a persistently high symptom level as an immediate acute warning. A sudden improvement could also warrant discussion rather than being interpreted automatically as resolution.
- Two kinds of reference were considered important: comparison with the individual’s own baseline and comparison with validated norms from a broader healthy population. A personal baseline alone may normalize an already concerning pattern if monitoring begins after symptoms have been present for a long time.
- The team asked whether clinicians would want standard indicators for all users or personalized monitoring configurations. Clinicians noted that needs may differ between diagnostic assessment and ongoing treatment: assessment may require a broad picture of current status, while treatment monitoring may emphasize change over time and response to therapy.
- An example of a symptom-tracking clinical app, **Robin Z**, was mentioned. Its clinician dashboard allows mood and other symptom trajectories to be reviewed with adolescents, which clinicians said can support discussion and shared interpretation.
- The developers mentioned an assistant feature under development that would use an **LLM** to answer questions about the data. This was presented as an exploratory interface idea, not a completed capability.

### 7. Collaboration, Analysis Support, and Funding Opportunity
- The clinical team asked for guidance on analyzing the passive-sensing data, including how to assess whether passive signals improve prediction beyond EMA and what analyses are feasible with a small sample.
- The technology team offered to support early analysis and suggested contacting **Clemens**, described as experienced in well-being research and smartphone data. The participants agreed that these avenues are complementary and that the circle of collaborators could be expanded.
- The technology team also expressed interest in demonstrating the system further and potentially exploring a deployment once the prototype is more stable.
- A possible **DIGIT Innovation Grant** was introduced. It supports digitalization-focused projects in the Zurich area. The clinical presenter said they had previously received a larger grant for a **3-year** project with a university of teacher education partner to digitize ADHD diagnosis and monitoring using **fNIRS** (functional near-infrared spectroscopy).
- The smaller grant discussed offers **€75,000 for 1 year**, with a deadline of **January 13** (the transcript does not specify the year; given the meeting date of November 20, 2026, the upcoming deadline would be January 13, 2027). The proposed application would be collaborative, with the clinical presenter potentially serving as applicant and including the other team members. The application was described as requiring a short written proposal and a video presentation. No decision to apply was recorded.

## Action Items
- **Clinical and technology teams:** Explore feasible initial analyses of the available smartphone passive-sensing data, taking account of the small sample and ongoing recruitment.
- **Clinical team:** Consider contacting Clemens for methodological guidance on smartphone and well-being data analysis.
- **Technology team:** Continue refining the network-data dashboard, particularly the balance between a concise clinician-facing overview and access to transparent supporting detail.
- **Technology team:** Consider how to represent sleep, social-service use, risk-related site visits, personal deviations, and population norms without overstating what the data establish.
- **Both teams:** Discuss whether and how router-based monitoring could be deployed in a clinic or at home, while addressing Wi-Fi coverage gaps, cellular-data limitations, consent, privacy, and regulatory requirements.
- **Both teams:** Consider a future demonstration or pilot deployment when the network-monitoring system is more stable.
- **Clinical presenter and collaborators:** Decide whether to pursue the DIGIT Innovation Grant before the **January 13** deadline and, if so, develop the short proposal and required video.

## Follow-Up Points
- Recruitment and data collection for the LiFE smartphone study are ongoing; the sample was still approximately **10 participants or fewer** during the meeting.
- Further collaboration is expected around passive-sensing analysis and possible system demonstrations, but no date for a follow-up meeting was specified.
- The teams may revisit the dashboard’s clinical indicators, presentation layers, personalized monitoring, and reference norms as the prototype and analyses develop.
- The potential grant application remains under consideration; no commitment or final project scope was established.
