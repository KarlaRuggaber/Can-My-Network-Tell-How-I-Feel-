# Meeting Summary

## Main Topics Discussed

### 1. Project context and proposed passive-sensing use case
- The team described ongoing work to show an anonymized, consistent IP address for connected devices. A future possibility would be a VPN connection back through the router for mobile use, allowing activity to be observed when a person is away from home; this would introduce latency and slower performance.
- The project idea was partly prompted by a conversation with the University of Zurich (UZH) earlier in the week. UZH has a program in which young people with mental-health difficulties are initially treated in an inpatient setting, then return to their usual environment while a phone app monitors usage and app interactions.
- The team sees network-level sensing as a possible complement to that app, especially because the current approach does not capture which apps are used. The participants mentioned psychiatrist Lukasz Migielski and peer worker Mia Bachmann as people involved in the UZH project.

### 2. User-facing experience and transparency
- The user view is intended to explain what is being collected and provide a high-level overview of usage patterns, including when devices were used and periods of network inactivity. A current label, “Quiet Window at Night,” may imply sleep even though the network only detects when devices stop sending traffic.
- Users can ask an LLM to explain observations, such as what was unusual. The team emphasized that the interface is informational rather than diagnostic: the data are only indicators and must be interpreted by a psychiatrist in the context of the person’s overall situation.
- A central motivation for the user-facing view is transparency: people should be able to see what is tracked and what information may be available to clinicians, rather than experiencing the system as a “black box.” The team described this as helping users feel informed and less exposed, rather than giving them direct control over the clinical process.
- The reviewer recommended simplifying the user view substantially. Many people find charts and multiple axes difficult to interpret; dense day/night visualizations may appear intimidating or invasive. Consider showing a simple first layer, with more detailed charts available on demand.
- Replace the prominent “Can my network tell how I feel?” heading with a neutral product name or straightforward wording. The current title sounds invasive; the team noted that “How I Feel” is the title of the project work, not necessarily the final product branding.
- The “What stands out?” panel may confuse users unless it clearly explains why something is highlighted. Remove it if the team cannot provide an understandable justification.
- For the user-facing chat, prioritize concise, directly relevant answers. In the example discussed, a question about the most unusual day should receive a brief answer grounded in the week overview, not an extensive explanation. Additional detail could be offered through controls such as “summarize,” “extend,” or follow-up questions.
- The team already transfers spoken onboarding input into a routine table. The reviewer suggested having the conversational system actively ask for missing routine information—such as daily activities and times—so the resulting profile is more complete.
- The timeline should also be reduced to information that answers clear user questions. Every chart, color, and visual element should have a specific purpose; unexplained tiles or color ranges risk increasing concerns about how much is being tracked.

### 3. Clinical dashboard and comparisons
- The clinical interface supports switching between people and associating devices with individuals through settings. The team showed a clinician overview, individual patient pages, metrics, timelines, explanations, notes, and a care-plan configuration view.
- The clinician overview is intended to surface what is new or noteworthy, because clinicians may already know a patient’s background when they enter a program. The system compares behavior both with the individual’s own baseline and with a broader reference group.
- For the current prototype, the “healthy people” comparison is approximated by averaging the baselines of the other two profiles, excluding the person being assessed. This is a temporary workaround because the team does not yet have a larger, time-aligned reference database.
- The reviewer recommended moving toward norm ranges rather than a single average. Potential methods include standard deviations, confidence intervals, or percentiles, with results expressed as whether a measure falls within or outside a reference range. A normal distribution’s central ±1 standard deviation covers approximately 68.3% of observations.
- Individual patterns vary: the discussion contrasted “morning larks” and “night owls” as ordinary differences that a system should not label abnormal. The clinical team should be consulted about suitable norms and potential data sources.
- Avoid “healthy people,” which can sound diagnostic. The reviewer suggested wording such as “typical” or describing a measure relative to a reference group—for example, “later nights than X percent of people.”
- The current selector shows three patients; the team should plan how the interface will scale to hundreds, perhaps through a searchable list or dropdown.
- Clinicians have limited time, so visual cues such as icons could help them rapidly identify cases or patterns needing attention. The team should ask clinicians which signals they consider important, including possible red flags such as high variability in sleep duration.

### 4. Clinical metrics, timelines, and visual design
- The clinical view includes personalized measures. In one example, the displayed set includes “Crisis or Self-Harm Sites” because the sample profile is configured around suicidality risk or intent. Other metrics are more general.
- Charts show an individual “usual range” and a separate reference range; the prototype also visually distinguishes an initial baseline period from a later clinical period. The scripted example uses an initial 14-day baseline, followed by the clinical period.
- The reviewer questioned the use of red for general activity intensity. Red commonly signals danger or a negative status, whereas the heatmap currently uses darker red simply to indicate more network packets. Use a neutral palette for activity levels and reserve red for clearly defined alerts or clinically significant flags. The clinical-period shading should likewise avoid implying danger if it only denotes a time period.
- The team showed model-oriented detail pages, including criteria such as sleep timing, sleep duration, and night/day traffic ratio, daily likelihood values, and a heatmap in which darker color represents greater intensity. The example included a 72% sleep-timing value on a day when the person was awake after 4 a.m.
- The reviewer found the statistical layer difficult to interpret without explanation, including standardized Z-values and weighting. The team should determine whether clinicians need this technical layer at all; a clearer explanation of which observed outliers contributed to a result may be more useful.
- Charts need clearly labeled axes, especially the currently unlabeled Y-axis. Legends, including the distinction between “usual range” and the reference range, should be more legible and explicit.
- The timeline enables clinicians to select a day, inspect details, and add notes—for example, to log known context for an observed change. The reviewer liked the ability to open details and add contextual notes.
- The interface also allows clinicians to view criteria not configured as risks for a particular person, inspect events such as crisis-line domain hits, and see what network data can and cannot reveal. Explanations should make clear that network data do not expose search terms or page contents.

### 5. LLM explanations, reliability, and privacy
- The system uses an LLM to explain data and model outputs. The team has focused on controlling framing and detail, since early responses were overly technical and included formulas that clinicians did not need.
- For direct questions about measured data, the LLM is intended to inspect the underlying data rather than invent values. However, the team has not yet thoroughly tested the accuracy of explanatory responses, so unsupported or incorrect explanations remain a risk.
- The reviewer raised the privacy concern of sending potentially clinical data to a US commercial model. The team agreed that a production-ready solution requires an in-house model and that the current external-model use is only a proof of concept, with a prominent disclaimer.
- The team is considering demonstrating a locally run model by using a laptop as an LLM server, since the small mini-PC may not have enough computing power. The reviewer supported demonstrating a local, constrained model tailored to the use case.
- For users, the LLM must avoid creating an interpretive or diagnostic “downward spiral.” It should stick to limited observations—for example, noting less sleep or more activity—without drawing clinical conclusions. The reviewer reiterated that the system is not diagnostically reliable and that clinician judgment is essential.

### 6. Care-plan configuration
- The care-plan view lets clinicians review a person’s routine, background, planned treatments, contextual information (such as travel in December), and selected criteria or risks to focus on.
- The reviewer encouraged the team to develop this collaboratively and iteratively with clinicians, asking what they need to monitor and what information from patient conversations would help interpret the data. The ability to add contextual information from a clinical conversation was viewed positively.

## Action Items
- Simplify and declutter the user-facing views; make detailed visualizations optional and ensure each visible element answers a clear question.
- Replace the “Can my network tell how I feel?” heading with neutral product branding or less invasive wording.
- Reassess whether “What stands out?” is useful to users; retain it only if the reason for each highlight can be explained clearly.
- Make user-facing LLM answers shorter and more relevant, with optional expansion or follow-up controls.
- Improve onboarding prompts so the system gathers missing routine details and fills in the routine table more completely.
- Rework clinical reference comparisons toward norm ranges, percentiles, or confidence intervals; investigate suitable external norm data and consult clinicians about available clinical statistics.
- Replace “healthy people” with neutral wording such as “typical” or an explicit percentile-based comparison.
- Plan a scalable patient-selection interface for larger caseloads and explore concise icons or visual flags based on clinician-defined priorities.
- Use neutral colors for ordinary usage intensity and time-period shading; reserve red for meaningful alerts. Clarify the chart legends, ranges, axes, and labels.
- Review whether the technical model/statistics layer is necessary for clinicians; consider replacing it with plain-language explanations of contributing observations.
- Test LLM explanations for factual reliability and ensure responses stay within the evidence available in the data.
- Demonstrate or further investigate a locally hosted, constrained LLM; clearly communicate the proof-of-concept status and limitations of the current commercial model.
- Continue validating the care-plan workflow with clinicians and incorporate contextual notes from patient conversations.
